\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The evaluation protocol, not the model, determines what an encrypted-traffic
classification number means. On one corpus we measure a swing of 0.418 macro-F1
--- from 0.992 to 0.574 --- with nothing changed but the partition, and we show
the mechanism directly: under the standard protocol, a decision tree on the
server address alone scores 0.999.

The correction is not simply ``stop splitting randomly''. On our second corpus
random splitting costs nothing, and what costs 0.19 is holding out servers. The
axis that matters is whichever grouping the label is nearly a function of, and
that depends on how the corpus was collected in a way invisible from its label
space or schema. We propose that the axis be chosen by measurement --- fit a
single-identifier probe per identifier per candidate protocol, and adopt the
protocol under which they all collapse --- and we release that diagnostic.

Evaluated this way, the field looks different. A 2016 statistical baseline sits
within 0.01 macro-F1 of our neural model on one corpus. On the other, four
independent neural architectures fall within 0.05 of each other while
gradient-boosted trees on thirteen flow statistics sit 0.23 above all of them.
Five of our own architectural claims fail their own pre-registered tests,
including one whose result held while its explanation did not: distance-based
rejection of unknown applications does beat softmax thresholding, but ablating
the metric training that we introduced to produce that advantage makes it
better, so the advantage belongs to the scoring rule and applies to any
encoder.

We release all splits, all results, the failed experiments, and the audit that
found them.
